Secondary effects — what less meat could free up

"if less meat is produced, some crops could go for food, maybe some land could be used to grow more stuff? how difficult would it be?"

The main self-sufficiency dashboard holds domestic production fixed and only varies demand across its four scenarios. This page asks a different question: if production actually followed demand downward under a lower-meat diet, what would that free up — and could it plausibly help Estonia's weakest category, vegetables (46% self-sufficient on the official basis, and falling under every alternative diet scenario)?

The answer splits into two parts with genuinely different confidence levels, kept deliberately separate below rather than presented as equally solid.

Tier 1 — feed grain and cropland no longer needed Solid arithmetic

Built entirely from data already in this project (Phase 6's feed-conversion model, the scenario demand ratios, and PM20's own 2024 barley yield) plus one stated assumption: that domestic production of poultry, eggs, and red meat scales down in proportion to lower domestic demand. Every other scenario in this project holds production fixed — this is the one place that convention is deliberately relaxed, as a hypothesis, not a measurement.

Estonia's barley is already a large net export crop (2024: 315.2kt produced vs. 177.1kt used domestically, 179.8kt exported), with animal feed as the dominant domestic use. That's why this isn't framed as "feed grain redirected to human food" — shuffling already-produced grain between feed-use and export doesn't change any self-sufficiency ratio. The only lever that can actually move a weak category like vegetables is the land itself growing something else — which is Tier 2.

Tier 2 — if that land grew vegetables instead Illustrative sketch

Everything below adds real, additional uncertainty on top of Tier 1's assumption: a generic, non-Estonia-specific yield benchmark, and no check at all on soil suitability, drainage, capital, labour, storage, or market absorption. Read this as "is the land arithmetic even in the right ballpark" — not as a projection of what would actually happen.

Vegetable farming is far more labour- and capital-intensive than growing feed barley — different equipment, irrigation, storage, harvest labour, and market channels. None of that is priced into this sketch. Estonia's actual achievable vegetable yield is also very plausibly lower than the EU-wide average used here, since that average is pulled up by large specialised producers (Netherlands, Spain) with longer growing seasons.

What this does and doesn't tell you

The striking number here is the last column of the Tier 2 table: under every scenario, closing Estonia's entire current vegetable production/use gap (81,068 t) would need only a small single-digit percentage of the land this analysis says would no longer be needed for feed. That's a genuinely useful finding on its own terms — it says the land arithmetic is not the binding constraint on vegetable self-sufficiency, at least not at this generic yield.

What it doesn't tell you is whether that land conversion would actually happen, or could happen quickly, or cheaply. Real constraints this sketch doesn't model at all: whether ex-feed-grain fields have the drainage and soil type vegetables need; the capital cost of new irrigation, storage, and harvest equipment; whether there's labour and market demand to absorb a large new vegetable harvest; and the multi-year lag any of this would take even if all the economics lined up. This project's own original plan flagged exactly this kind of production-capacity-and-land-reallocation modelling as out of scope for v1, for these reasons — this page is a bounded, clearly-labelled first look at it, not an attempt to fully answer it.
