07 — How this was built

Methodology & sources

Full methodology, data sources, and independent validation ▸

What this model is

A bottom-up comparison of two things, food group by food group: how much Estonia's population would need to eat to meet TAI (Tervise Arengu Instituut) nutritional recommendations, and how much of that Estonia actually produces. Built over nine phases — data acquisition, taxonomy reconciliation, a demographic requirement model, an actual-consumption model, a supply/self-sufficiency model, feed and waste adjustments, a two-scenario engine, independent validation, and this dashboard.

Scope: national aggregate, current production levels — this measures self-sufficiency at today's output, not at Estonia's potential output. No seasonality, regional, or shock-resilience modelling. Ages 2 and up (infants excluded — infant feeding doesn't fit the toidupüramiid framework this model is built around).

How the numbers are built

Requirement model (recommended demand). Population × age/sex-specific energy needs × TAI's portion-by-energy-level guidance = tonnes/year, if everyone ate exactly as recommended. Physical activity level has no Estonian population-distribution data, so a single "moderate" default is used nationally rather than an invented split.

Consumption model (actual demand). The same demographic grid, weighted by RTU011's measured grams/day. RTU011 bundles poultry with red meat and offal into one figure — split using the production side's own per-capita consumption shares, not absolute levels. Fruit and berries, and nuts and seeds/cocoa, can only be measured combined in every Estonian source used.

Self-sufficiency (production ÷ domestic use). Built bottom-up from the PM-series balance sheets, calibrated against the official strategy-document figures. When the two diverge by more than a few points, the official (multi-year) figure is used as the headline and the derived figure is kept for transparency, not discarded.

Feed dependency. Animal products are only as domestic as their feed. Generic, published feed-conversion-ratio benchmarks (not Estonia-specific) against known domestic feed supply give roughly 82% aggregate feed coverage — with the important caveat that the shortfall concentrates in imported protein concentrates, not forage, which the aggregate figure alone hides. Feed-adjusted lower bounds use illustrative haircuts, shown as sensitivities, never as measurements.

Waste. Estonia's 2021 national waste study, allocated to food groups. Losses before the household inflate how much needs to be produced for a given amount actually eaten; household waste specifically is modelled as a separate, cuttable lever that reduces need without requiring more production.

Scenario B. Self-sufficiency is re-scaled by the ratio of Scenario A to Scenario B demand, production held fixed — an approximation that assumes proportional consistency between the two demand models, not a fully independent re-derived balance sheet. Flagged wherever it's used.

Independent validation against FAOSTAT

Estonia's 2022 FAOSTAT Food Balance Sheet (the latest year FAOSTAT publishes) was cross-checked commodity by commodity against this project's figures. Six categories agreed closely — wheat/rye, potato, fruit, eggs, fish, and pork — despite different years and methodologies, a meaningful corroboration of the overall approach. Four divergences were investigated and documented rather than resolved with false confidence: vegetables (a third disagreeing data point on an already-flagged gap), beef and poultry (plausibly real year-to-year shifts), and — most substantively — rapeseed, where FAOSTAT confirmed a fragility this project had already flagged: the "yield cancellation" shortcut used for oil self-sufficiency breaks down because Estonia crushes and re-exports a large share of its rapeseed oil directly, meaning the project's 69.3% oil figure likely understates true self-sufficiency. Legumes' long-standing "unknown" gap was revised to a bimodal finding: Estonia is a large net exporter of field peas (mostly feed and export use) but near-zero self-sufficient in the dry beans and lentils people actually eat.

Assumptions & limitations, stated plainly

The consumption baseline is a 2014 survey — the best available, cross-checked where possible against fresher signals, and flagged as dated throughout. Self-sufficiency ratios lean on an official pre-computed average as calibration rather than a fully independent build for every category. Feed-conversion, feed-import-share, and waste-lever figures are generic, published benchmarks, not Estonia-specific measurements — always shown as labelled sensitivities. There is no production-capacity-ceiling modelling: "self-sufficiency" means at today's output, not Estonia's potential output. Micronutrient adequacy is a qualitative flag, not a quantitative model — vegetables, fruit and fish are simultaneously the weakest self-sufficiency categories and disproportionately important sources of vitamin C, folate, and omega-3/iodine, which strengthens rather than separates from the self-sufficiency story. Reference years don't line up exactly across sources (RTU011 2014, PM-series 2024, FAOSTAT 2022, SEI waste 2021, official strategy figures ~2018–2022) — genuinely part of why some cross-checks in this dashboard show the divergences they do.

Two genuine data gaps were reported as ranges or left unresolved rather than forced into false-precision points: porridge/pasta/rice/grain products (self-sufficiency somewhere between 0% and 178%+, no consumption-mix data to weight barley against rice and durum pasta), and legumes (bimodal — see above). Sweets and snacks' self-sufficiency is structurally near-zero for raw sugar (no domestic refining) and not scoreable on a like-for-like basis for manufactured goods.
